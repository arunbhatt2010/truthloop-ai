I Lost 82% of My Traffic While Building the Product

Here is the uncomfortable part of building a startup: sometimes the thing you are working hardest on is not the thing that needs fixing.

I recently looked at my website analytics and saw something difficult to ignore.

May 15 – Jun 13 4.84K users
Jun 14 – Jul 13 3K users
Jul 14 – Aug 12 1.23K users
Aug 13 – Sep 11 846 users

That is a drop from 4.84K to 846 monthly active users. Roughly an 82% decline.

At first, the obvious reaction is: How do I get the traffic back?

But that was not the most useful question.

The Easy Problem Was Traffic

Traffic feels like a clean problem.

You can publish more articles. You can post more on LinkedIn. You can comment more. You can improve SEO. You can work on social distribution.

All of those things are useful.

But they can also become a very convenient distraction.

A traffic problem is often easier to think about than an execution problem.

What Was I Actually Doing?

While the traffic was falling, I was spending a lot of time building.

I was improving the product. I was working on the investigation flow. I was fixing systems. I was thinking about evidence. I was working on Loop 7. I was solving technical problems that felt urgent.

From the inside, it looked like progress.

And that is what made it dangerous.

Busy work can feel productive because something is always moving.

The harder question is:

Was I building because the product needed it, or because building was easier than facing distribution?

Building Can Become a Form of Avoidance

Founders rarely say:

"I am avoiding distribution."

They say:

  • "This feature still needs improvement."
  • "The system is not ready yet."
  • "We need better evidence."
  • "We should fix this first."
  • "Then we can focus on growth."

Sometimes those statements are completely correct.

But sometimes they create a loop:

Build → Fix → Improve → Research → Build again → Delay distribution.

The work is real. The problems are real. The effort is real.

But the outcome can still move in the wrong direction.

The Numbers Were Not Lying

The analytics did not tell me why the traffic was falling.

They simply showed the result.

That distinction matters.

Data can tell you what happened. It does not automatically tell you which pattern created it.

That is where many founders get stuck.

They collect more information when what they actually need is better diagnosis.

The Difference Between Advice and Diagnosis

Advice says:

"Post more."

"Improve SEO."

"Launch faster."

"Talk to more users."

Diagnosis asks a different question:

Why does the same problem keep returning even after I know what I should do?

That is the question behind TruthLoop AI.

Instead of giving another generic productivity answer, TruthLoop is designed to investigate recurring hesitation, overthinking, avoidance, and decision patterns.

From Conversation to Evidence

The important part is not only what a founder says.

It is also what repeatedly happens.

That is why TruthLoop looks at two sides:

  • What you reveal during the investigation.
  • What your public behavior and digital footprint reveal over time.

The goal is to compare the story with the evidence.

Sometimes they match.

Sometimes they do not.

And that gap can be extremely useful.

The Real Lesson From My Traffic Drop

I still need more traffic.

I still need better distribution.

I still need to publish more useful content.

Those things are true.

But the deeper lesson is different.

Sometimes the bottleneck is not the strategy. It is the pattern that keeps making you search for another strategy.

That is why a founder can know exactly what to do and still not do it.

The problem is not always information.

Sometimes it is the hidden reason the information never becomes action.

What I Would Do Differently Now

I would watch three things together:

  • What the analytics say.
  • What I say I am prioritizing.
  • What I repeatedly spend my time doing.

The interesting part is the gap between those three.

If I say distribution is the priority, but most of my week goes into product fixes, the pattern is worth investigating.

Not judging. Not blaming. Investigating.

Sometimes the Metric Is Not the Problem

A falling metric gets your attention.

But the metric may only be the visible symptom.

The deeper problem may be the repeated behavior behind it.

That is true for traffic. It is true for launches. It is true for hiring. It is true for product decisions. It is true for founders who keep saying:

"I know what I should do. I just keep not doing it."

The Next Step

I am not going to pretend that one analytics screenshot explains everything.

It does not.

But it can be enough to ask a better question.

Not: "How do I get more traffic?"

But: "What pattern might be causing me to repeatedly lose momentum?"

That question is much harder.

It is also much more useful.
